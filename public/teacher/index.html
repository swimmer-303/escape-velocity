Escape Velocity: Teacher Guide

A free browser game where students run a base on Mars across three crews. They make real engineering trade-offs about power, air, food, radiation and rocket fuel, and real NASA space-weather data drives the solar storms.

Open the game Print this guide

At a glance. Ages 7 to 12 (grades 2 to 7). 45 to 55 minutes. Works on Chromebooks, laptops and tablets in any modern browser: no download, no accounts. The optional teacher dashboard keeps only nicknames and game results, and deletes them after 7 days. Students can play alone, in pairs, or all together as one class in Mission Control (see below). The campaign saves after every turn in the browser, so a class can stop and continue on another day on the same device.

What students learn

  • Engineering trade-offs: every power cell can do only one job. Spending power on safety, food or building today means less rocket fuel today. There is no "free" choice.
  • Investing for the future: upgrades cost power now and pay off later, and they are inherited by the next crew. Students feel why long-term planning matters.
  • Real Mars constraints: thin sunlight, dust storms, freezing nights, radiation from solar storms, and messages from Earth that take minutes to arrive.
  • How scientists use data: the solar storms in the game are real events from NASA's DONKI database, and the game's Science Log shows exactly where each number comes from.

Lesson plan (45 to 55 minutes)

5 minHook. Ask: "If you lived on Mars, what would you need to stay alive?" List answers. Then ask: "And how would you get home?" Explain that the same machine that makes air on Mars can also make rocket fuel, so astronauts have to choose.

Short on time? Use Quick mission (one crew, about 10 minutes). For a science-history lesson, try May 2024 storms: the real solar storms of May 2024 hit the base on their real dates, ending with the far-side eruption that NASA's Curiosity rover measured on May 20, 2024.

Class codes: everyone plays the same Mars

A class code is a short code like K7RZQ. When every student uses the same code, the whole class gets the same Mars, so students can compare their choices fairly: "We all had the same dust storm on sol 6. What did you do?"

  • Make a code: open the game, choose New campaign (or Quick mission), and press Make a code for my class. The code appears in big letters. Codes never use O, I, L, 0, 1 or vowels, so they are easy to read aloud and copy.
  • Share it: write the code on the board, or press Copy link and post the link (it looks like .../?code=K7RZQ). Students who open the link have the code filled in for them.
  • Students enter it: on the New campaign or Quick mission screen, they type the code under "Got a code from your teacher?" (capitals, spaces and dashes don't matter), then everyone picks the same difficulty. Quick missions are always Cadet.
  • The code stays with the game: it shows in the Menu and on the end screen ("Class code K7RZQ: compare your stars and choices with classmates"), and it is saved with the campaign, so it is still there after Continue.
The same for everyone with the codeWhat can still differ
The dust storms, extra-cold nights, cargo lander day, the radio blackout (Commander), and which real NASA solar storms hit and on which sol. The same choices give exactly the same results. Each student's choices, so their air, food, fuel, radiation dose, stars and base will differ: that is the point of comparing. Also the solar storms, in two cases: NASA adds new events to its live list over time, and a device that cannot reach NASA uses the game's stored archive instead. So use a code during the same class period, and check that every screen shows the same data badge (LIVE or ARCHIVE) at the top.

A class code is not an account: it decides what the Mars weather does. If you made the code with the button, each student's game also sends its results to your private teacher dashboard (below): a nickname and game numbers, nothing else.

Teacher dashboard: the whole class at a glance

When you press Make a code for my class, the game also gives you a private Teacher dashboard link. It shows how every crew in your class did, live, so you can lead the discussion with real results.

How to open it

  • Under the big class code, press Copy next to "Teacher dashboard" and keep the link somewhere private (or press Open). Open it on your laptop or on the projector. It checks for new results every 10 seconds.
  • Once the dashboard is open, its secret part is hidden from the address bar, so you can project it. To open it again later, use the link you copied. On the computer that made the code, the class code box also shows a link to it.
  • After each crew, students' games send the result by themselves. The first time, the end screen asks each student for a nickname for the class board. Students can press Skip: they then show up as "Player 3".
  • If the game's server can't be reached, class codes work exactly as before, just without a dashboard.

What it shows

  • Class summary: how many students, crews launched and crews sent home early, and the average stars per crew.
  • Most common trouble: why crews came home early (air, food, radiation, or out of time), with a question to ask the class for each one, such as "Many crews ran out of air. Why do you think that happened?"
  • Most built upgrades: how many students finished each upgrade.
  • Students: a table with each student's site, crews, stars, fastest launch, what they built and how their last crew ended. Press a column heading to sort.
  • Fuel tank lines: a small chart of each student's last crew, from landing to launch (or not).
  • Buttons: Hide names (everyone shows as "Player 1, 2, 3..." for projecting), Full screen, and Manage, to take a student off the board or delete every result now.

Privacy

  • Students give only a first name or nickname (up to 12 letters and numbers, through a word filter), or nothing at all. No accounts, no email, no free text.
  • Only the nickname and the game numbers are kept: no addresses or other information. Only someone with your dashboard link can see the board.
  • The board and every result are deleted 7 days after the last result, or right away with Manage, Delete every result now.

Mars or the Moon

When students start a campaign or a Quick mission, they choose where to build: Mars (Gale Crater) or the Moon (near the lunar south pole, the region NASA plans to explore with Artemis). The May 2024 storms scenario is Mars only. On the Moon:

  • One turn = one Earth day, and each mission has a lunar night: the solar panels make nothing and the heaters need more. A real lunar night lasts about 14.8 Earth days (half of the 29.53-day cycle from one sunrise to the next); the game squeezes it into 4 days, and says so. Students have to stock up air and food beforehand, and the battery and the nuclear reactor matter more.
  • Oxygen from rocks: the Moon has no air, so the O2 plant takes oxygen out of the soil. Moon soil (regolith) is 40 to 45% oxygen by weight, and in 2023 NASA extracted oxygen from simulated lunar soil in a vacuum chamber. It is the same air-or-fuel trade-off.
  • More radiation, faster warnings: China's Chang'e 4 lander measured about 1.37 mSv per day on the lunar surface, about twice the Mars value, so every day costs more dose (Moon crews get a bigger game budget). But Earth is only about 1.3 light-seconds away, so storm warnings always arrive in time, and there is no blackout.
  • Escape velocity is about 2.4 km/s, less than half of Mars' 5.0 km/s, so a Moon rocket needs less fuel.

Compare and contrast: have half the class play Mars and half the Moon, then share. Which place was harder? Why does a two-week night change what you build? Why do warnings arrive late on Mars but not on the Moon?

Choose a landing site (Mars)

On Mars, students also pick a landing site: Gale Crater (where NASA's Curiosity rover explores, near the equator; the default) or Arcadia Planitia, icy plains about 47° north. NASA's Subsurface Water Ice Mapping project (SWIM) combined data from three Mars orbiters and found that Arcadia's plains show the most signs of ice that astronauts could dig up. The game shows SWIM's real map value for the top meter of ground: about +0.55 at Arcadia and about 0 at Gale, on a scale from -1 (the data say no ice) to +1 (every clue says ice).

  • The upside: ice means water, so each greenhouse cell grows twice the food and air.
  • The downside: farther north there is less sunshine (1 fewer power cell every sol) and more cold nights. The first crew has a harder start; bigger crews later benefit more from cheap food.
  • The ice map and the "ice or sunshine" trade-off are real; how much each effect changes the game is a game choice, and the Science Log says so.

Compare and contrast: have half the class land at Gale Crater and half at Arcadia Planitia with the same class code, then share. Which crew launched sooner? Which one struggled with food, and which with power? Real mission planners call the mid-latitudes a "middle ground" between the most ice (near the poles) and the most sunshine (near the equator). Where would you send the first astronauts, and what would you build first to make up for what your site lacks?

Mission Control (team play): the whole class runs one base

In Mission Control your projector shows the base, and every student works one station from their own Chromebook, tablet or phone. All stations share the same few power cells each sol, so the class has to agree on a plan: exactly the kind of negotiation real mission controllers do.

How to run it

  1. On the projector: open the game, press Class mission: Mission Control, then Start a room for my class. Pick a difficulty (Cadet is best for classes) and a length (one crew, or three crews where the base carries over), and press Open the room. A big room code appears, like K7RZQ.
  2. Students join: they open the game, press Class mission, and type the room code and their first name or a nickname. Or share the link under the code (.../?room=K7RZQ): the code is filled in for them.
  3. Check the stations: the lobby shows who is at each station. Press Share out stations to even them out, or Manage players to move a student or remove a name. Then press Start the mission.
  4. Each sol: read the morning briefing aloud and press Start planning. Students place cells in their own station's modules and press I'm ready. When every cell is placed, press Run sol (or turn on Auto-run to run it as soon as everyone connected is ready). Everyone watches the day play out, then reads the debrief together. Press Start sol for the next one.

  • Everyone sees the whole plan, the meters and the shared pool of cells, but can only move cells in their own station. If O2 wants more fuel, someone else has to give a cell back.
  • Big classes share stations: a class of 25 is about 6 students per station. With fewer than 4 students, stations are combined (2 students run 2 stations each). Students who join late go to the station with the fewest players.
  • There is no chat. Students tap preset messages such as "I need more cells!", "Storm warning: shelter!" or "Wait, let's talk!", which appear with their name on every screen for a few seconds. The real talking happens out loud, in the room.

Timing

Allow about 5 minutes to join and check stations. One crew takes about 15 to 25 minutes (a sol takes 1 to 2 minutes once the class gets going); three crews take 45 to 60 minutes. Stop for discussion after the first few sols: that is when the negotiating gets interesting.

Talk about it: sharing one power budget

  • Which station wanted the most cells? How did your class decide who got them?
  • Was there a sol when one station had to give up cells for another? Was that fair? Was it the right call?
  • When a storm warning came in, who changed their plan? Who had to give something up?
  • Is it better for one person to decide, or for every station to agree? What did you do when you disagreed?
  • Real mission controllers split jobs between stations too. Why is it hard to share one power budget?

Safety and privacy

  • Students use a first name or nickname only (up to 12 letters and numbers). A small word filter blocks unkind names, and you can remove any player in Manage players; a removed name cannot rejoin that room.
  • No accounts, no chat, and nothing is saved: the room lives only in the game server's memory. It closes when you press Close the room, or after 3 hours with nothing happening.
  • If a student's page reloads, it rejoins the same seat automatically. If their device restarts, they can join again with a slightly different name (for example "Ava 2"); greyed-out players are not connected and never hold up the class.

How the game works (for you)

  • One turn = one sol (a Mars day, 24 hours 39 minutes). Each sol, students get a number of power cells from the solar panels and must place every cell.
  • O2 Plant: cells on AIR make air to breathe; cells on FUEL fill the rocket. Same machine, two jobs. This is based on NASA's MOXIE experiment on the Perseverance rover.
  • Greenhouse makes food; heaters keep the batteries from freezing (skipping them costs power the next day); the storm shelter blocks radiation from solar storms, but only when it is powered.
  • Workshop: cells here build upgrades that last for every later crew: more solar panels, a radiation monitor (early storm warnings), a battery, a soil berm for the shelter, a second greenhouse dome, a bigger O2 plant, and a nuclear reactor.
  • Three meters: Power, Life Support (days of air and food), and Radiation dose. The goal: fill the Fuel Tank before the launch window closes.
  • Failing is part of learning. If air or food runs out or the dose budget is used up, the crew simply heads home early, the game explains why in plain words, and the base (and the campaign) continues.

Vocabulary

SolA day on Mars: 24 hours and 39 minutes.
Solar flareA sudden burst of energy from the Sun. Classes go from small to big: C, M, X.
Radiation doseHow much radiation a person's body absorbs. Measured in sieverts (Sv) or millisieverts (mSv).
Solar conjunctionWhen the Sun is between Earth and Mars and blocks radio messages for a couple of weeks.
Light timeHow long a message takes to travel at the speed of light. Earth to Mars: about 3 to 22 minutes. Earth to the Moon: about 1.3 seconds.
Lunar nightThe Moon's night: about 14.8 Earth days with no sunlight. Lunar daylight lasts just as long.
Propellant / oxidizerWhat a rocket burns. Most of a Mars rocket's propellant would be oxygen.
RegolithThe loose soil and dust on Mars or the Moon. Piled up thick, it blocks radiation. Moon regolith is 40 to 45% oxygen by weight.
Escape velocityThe speed needed to break free of a planet's or moon's gravity. Mars: about 5 km/s. The Moon: about 2.4 km/s.

The real science and data

  • Solar storms: real flares and particle storms from NASA DONKI (ccmc.gsfc.nasa.gov/tools/DONKI), fetched live when possible and labeled LIVE or ARCHIVE in the game.
  • Message delay: calculated from JPL's orbital elements for today's date; it matches JPL Horizons.
  • Radiation: Curiosity rover measurements on Mars, Chang'e 4 lander measurements on the Moon (Zhang et al., Science Advances, 2020), and ISS measurements from NASA's RadLab database.
  • Ice on Mars: NASA's Subsurface Water Ice Mapping project (SWIM, swim.psi.edu; Morgan et al., Nature Astronomy, 2021) for the Arcadia Planitia landing site, and NASA Mars Trek elevations for its flat plains.
  • Moon facts: the NASA Moon Fact Sheet (nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov), ESA (oxygen in regolith) and NASA's 2023 Carbothermal Reduction Demonstration.
  • Space biology: Real Science cards link to real NASA OSDR studies (plants, radiation, bone and muscle).
  • What we simplified: the game speeds up time, uses smaller amounts of fuel, and uses a much stricter radiation budget than NASA's real limit, so that choices matter in a short game. The in-game Science Log lists every simplification.

From Mars to Earth: sustainability

Every crew lives within hard limits: a small power budget, no water to waste, food grown under lights. At the end of each mission, the game links those choices to NASA technology that already helps people and the planet on Earth: water recycling from the space station, indoor vertical farming that grew out of NASA's plant research, and giant batteries, first invented by NASA, that store solar power for communities. Each link cites a NASA Spinoff article.

  • What did your crew have to recycle or save? Where do we waste the same things at home or at school?
  • Indoor farms grow food with less water and no soil. Where might that help most on Earth?
  • Why does storing solar power matter for a Mars base, and for a town during a storm?

Extension ideas

  • Math: one power cell on AIR makes air for 4 people for a day. How many cells does a crew of 6 need each day? Of 10?
  • Data: open DONKI and find the biggest solar flare this month. What class was it?
  • Writing: write a letter from Crew 1 to Crew 2 explaining what they built and why.
  • Design: draw a Mars base. What would you build first, and what does it cost?
